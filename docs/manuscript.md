# Observation-process features and cross-hospital transportability of hourly sepsis early-warning models: a bidirectional external validation study

*Draft. Every number in Results, Abstract and Tables comes from `results_confirm/summary.json`, `results_confirm/*/fs_full_decomposition.json`, `results_confirm/subgroups.json` and `results/data_audit.json`; tables are generated by `experiments/make_confirm_tables.py` (`docs/manuscript_tables.md`).*

## Abstract

**Objective.** To test whether observation-process features (whether, when and how often variables are measured) improve hourly sepsis early-warning models within a hospital at the cost of transportability to another hospital.

**Methods.** Bidirectional external validation using the two public hospital systems of the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2019 (A: 20,336 patients, 8.8% septic; B: 20,000 patients, 5.7% septic; Sepsis-3 labels). Four model classes (logistic regression, LightGBM, GRU, fuzzy scorecard) were each trained with and without explicit process features under an equal tuning budget. The primary estimand was the difference-in-differences (DiD) in AUROC between locally trained and transferred models on identical target patients, with patient-level bootstrap intervals and Holm correction over 8 tests. Secondary analyses covered clinical utility, calibration, patient-level conformal prediction and label-free prevalence estimation.

**Results.** The DiD was positive in 7 of 8 class × direction combinations and significant after correction in 3 (logistic regression +0.064 and +0.055; GRU +0.042 from A to B). Process features raised local AUROC by up to 0.06 but gave no transferred model an advantage from A to B, and lowered transferred AUROC for the GRU (−0.024) and the fuzzy scorecard (−0.040). Process terms accounted for 63–75% of the change in mean model output between hospitals. Source-calibrated conformal sets (nominal 90%) covered 78–82% of septic hours after transfer from A to B and 62–83% of non-septic hours from B to A. Label-free prevalence estimators failed in both directions.

**Conclusion.** Within-hospital gains from observation-process features largely do not transport across hospitals, and source-calibrated uncertainty does not compensate. Claims that such features improve sepsis early-warning models should be supported by evaluation at a second site on the same patients as a locally trained model.

## 1. Introduction

Hourly sepsis early-warning models are typically developed on electronic health record (EHR) data from one institution and deployed at others. EHR data record not only patient physiology but also the care process that generated them: whether, when and how often a variable was measured. In a two-hospital Boston cohort, the timing of laboratory test orders predicted survival more accurately than the test results for 118 of 174 test types (Agniel et al., 2018). Such observation-process signals are, by construction, tied to local workflows. The PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2019 showed that algorithms predicting sepsis hours before clinical recognition generalized poorly to a hospital system unseen during development (Reyna et al., 2020). Recent work found that adding measurement counts to sepsis *mortality* models enlarged the performance drop between MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD (Yamamoto et al., 2026, preprint). Whether the same holds for *hourly early-warning* models has not been quantified, and neither has its consequence for decision thresholds and distribution-free uncertainty sets. In early-warning models, process features vary within a stay and are updated every hour.

We ask whether observation-process features improve within-hospital discrimination at the cost of cross-hospital transportability. We compare four model classes with and without process features, in both transfer directions between the two public hospital systems of the Challenge. Each transfer is evaluated against a model trained at the target hospital on the same patients.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Data source and participants
We used the publicly available training sets of the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2019 (v1.0.0; Reyna et al., 2020; Goldberger et al., 2000), comprising 20,336 ICU patients from hospital system A and 20,000 from hospital system B. Files were obtained from PhysioNet's official open-data bucket and each file was verified against the MD5 checksum published with it. Patient, septic-patient, row and entry counts reproduced Table 2 of Reyna et al. (2020) exactly. The data are de-identified and distributed under the Open Database License. The data contain hourly values of 8 vital signs and 26 laboratory tests, age, sex, hospital-to-ICU admission time, and hours since ICU admission (ICULOS). Administrative ICU-unit identifiers were not used.

### 2.2 Outcome
The outcome is the Challenge label: sepsis onset per Sepsis-3 (the earlier of suspected infection and a ≥2-point SOFA increase, within the Challenge's time window constraints), with the label set to 1 from 6 hours before onset onwards. Predictions are made every hour using only information available up to that hour. In >97% of septic patients the record ends 8–10 hours after the label becomes positive; we report this data-construction property and do not alter it.

### 2.3 Study design
Each hospital's patients were split into training, validation and test sets (70/15/15%), stratified by whether the patient became septic, with a fixed seed. For each direction (A→B, B→A), models were trained on the source training set. Tuning, early stopping, the decision threshold and conformal calibration used only the source validation set. Each model was evaluated on the source test set (internal) and on the target test set (external). For the primary comparison, the external model is contrasted with the same model class and variant trained at the target hospital, evaluated on the same target test patients.

### 2.4 Predictors and model variants
We compared four model classes. Each came in a FULL variant and a PROCESS-FREE (PF) variant:

- (i) L2-regularized logistic regression (LR) and (ii) LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) on 113 engineered features: last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) values, hours since each variable was last measured, 6-hour window statistics of vital signs, static covariates, ICULOS and the number of laboratory tests measured so far.
- (iii) A gated recurrent unit network (GRU; hidden size tuned) on LOCF values, ever-measured flags and log hours since measurement.
- (iv) A fuzzy scorecard: an additive model on learned Gaussian fuzzy memberships (LOW/NORMAL/HIGH) of each variable's LOCF value, their slow and fast exponential moving averages ("sustained", "rising"), and fuzzy memberships of the static covariates. In the FULL variant, memberships decay towards a learned population prior as a measurement ages, and "recently measured"/"frequently measured" terms are included. The additive form yields an exact decomposition of any change in mean logit into per-term contributions.

FULL variants encode the observation process explicitly (time since measurement, measurement indicators or counts, native missing-value handling in LightGBM). PF variants receive only values: LOCF values, window statistics of values, static covariates and ICULOS, with never-measured variables imputed by the source-training median and no missingness indicators. PF removes explicit process encodings but not residual process information carried by LOCF.

### 2.5 Model training and tuning
All classes received an equal, pre-specified tuning grid (LR: 3 configurations; LightGBM: 8; GRU: 4; fuzzy scorecard: 4). The criterion was source-validation log-loss, which was also used for early stopping (neural models: maximum 30 epochs, patience 5; LightGBM: 100 rounds). No class-imbalance correction was applied, to preserve calibration. The selected configuration was refit with five random seeds (LR: once). Predictions are the mean logit over seeds.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis
The analysis plan was fixed before the confirmatory run (Supplementary analysis plan). It was written after an exploratory analysis of the same data, which we disclose.

*Primary estimand.* For each model class and direction s→t, on the target test patients:
DiD = [AUROC_loc(FULL) − AUROC_ext(FULL)] − [AUROC_loc(PF) − AUROC_ext(PF)].
Here AUROC_loc is the model trained at t and AUROC_ext the model trained at s. A positive DiD means process features lose more discrimination on transfer. Inference used 1,000 patient-level bootstrap resamples of the target test set, with all predictions resampled jointly, percentile 95% confidence intervals and two-sided bootstrap p-values. The Holm procedure was applied across the eight class × direction tests.

*Secondary analyses (exploratory, unadjusted).*
- The external difference FULL − PF in AUROC and in the Challenge's normalized utility. The threshold for utility maximized utility on source validation.
- Calibration slope and calibration-in-the-large on the target.
- Class-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal prediction at α = 0.1. Calibration used one uniformly drawn hour per source-validation patient and class, so that calibration units are exchangeable patients. Coverage was estimated by averaging 200 draws of one hour per target patient.
- Target prevalence estimated without labels by black-box shift estimation (Lipton et al., 2018) and by EM (Saerens et al., 2002).

*Sensitivity analyses.*
- S1: excluding patients whose label was positive at their first hour.
- S2: removing ICULOS from all inputs (seed 0).
- S3: utility at the target-optimal threshold.

Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024).

### 2.7 Software and reproducibility
Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.14, LightGBM 4.7 and scikit-learn 1.9; CPU only. The utility metric is a vectorized re-implementation tested for exact agreement with the official Challenge scorer. All code, the data manifest and the analysis plan are available in the project repository.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Cohorts
Hospital A contributed 20,336 patients, of whom 1,790 (8.8%) became septic; 17,136 of 790,215 patient-hours (2.17%) were label-positive. Hospital B contributed 20,000 patients, 1,142 (5.7%) septic, with 10,780 of 761,995 hours (1.41%) positive. Median ICU length of stay was 39 h (IQR 25–47) in A and 38 h (23–47) in B; median age was 64.7 and 62.0 years, and 58.2% and 53.7% were male. Measurement practice differed markedly between the hospitals. The proportion of hours with a measurement, A relative to B, was ×45 for base excess, ×43 for bicarbonate and ×13.5 for chloride. Troponin I was measured ~15 times more often in B. End-tidal CO₂ was never recorded in A but recorded at least once in 16% of B patients. The target test sets comprised 3,002 patients (172 septic) in B and 3,052 (269 septic) in A.

### 3.2 Model selection
Selected configurations and the complete tuning logs are in the Supplement (`*_tuning.json`). Two points affect interpretation. First, logistic regression selected the smallest regularization value on the grid (C = 0.01) in all four variant × direction combinations, so the grid may not have contained the optimum. Second, the FULL fuzzy scorecard trained on A varied across seeds (internal AUROC SD 0.034 across five seeds, versus ≤0.018 for all other variants).

### 3.3 Primary estimand
The DiD estimate was positive in 7 of 8 model class × direction combinations (Table 2, Figure 1), i.e. process features lost more discrimination on transfer than their absence did. After Holm correction it was significant for logistic regression in both directions (A→B +0.064, 95% CI +0.033 to +0.098; B→A +0.055, +0.033 to +0.078) and for the GRU from A to B (+0.042, +0.019 to +0.066). LightGBM showed a small, consistent DiD in both directions (+0.014 each), significant before (A→B p = 0.03) but not after correction. The fuzzy scorecard showed +0.036 from A to B (Holm p = 0.06) and −0.011 (−0.028 to +0.007) from B to A. The GRU from B to A showed no effect (+0.002).

The pattern arises because process features raised *local* discrimination substantially in several classes, but the gain did not persist after transfer. For example, logistic regression trained at the target reached 0.813 (FULL) versus 0.754 (PF) at hospital B, whereas the transferred models reached 0.711 and 0.716. Directly comparing the transferred models (Table 3), FULL was not better than PF in any class from A to B, and was worse for the GRU (−0.024) and the fuzzy scorecard (−0.040). From B to A, FULL was better for the fuzzy scorecard (+0.018) and not different for the other classes.

### 3.4 Clinical utility, calibration and uncertainty
DiD in normalized utility was positive in 7 of 8 combinations (Table 3). In the direct comparison of transferred models, utility favoured PF for the fuzzy scorecard from A to B (−0.121 for FULL) and FULL was worse for logistic regression from B to A (−0.095). All other utility differences had confidence intervals including zero.

Calibration deteriorated on transfer for most models (Table 4): external observed/expected ratios were 0.59–0.98 from A to B and 0.61–1.22 from B to A, and external calibration slopes were 0.62–1.07 (A→B) and 0.56–0.88 (B→A), with no consistent advantage for either variant. The FULL fuzzy scorecard was best calibrated from A to B (O/E 0.98, slope 0.96).

Patient-level conformal prediction, calibrated at the source hospital with a nominal 90% level, lost validity on transfer. The direction of the failure depended on the direction of transfer (Table 4, Figure 2).
- From A to B, coverage of septic hours fell to 0.78–0.82 for FULL variants and to 0.85–0.93 for PF variants (PF higher in all four classes).
- From B to A, coverage of septic hours was preserved (0.86–0.95), but coverage of non-septic hours fell to 0.62–0.83, i.e. more alarms. PF was higher for logistic regression (0.79 vs 0.62) and LightGBM (0.70 vs 0.66) and lower for the GRU and the fuzzy scorecard.

Label-free estimation of the target prevalence failed (Table 5). From A to B (true 0.014), BBSE returned 0.000 for 7 of 8 models and EM returned 0.000 or 0.06–0.10. From B to A (true 0.022), both methods overestimated by 1.1- to 26-fold.

### 3.5 Sensitivity analyses
Excluding patients positive at their first hour (S1) left all estimates in the same direction, with similar magnitude (Table 6). Removing ICULOS (S2, single seed) kept the DiD positive in 7 of 8 combinations (one essentially zero: GRU from A to B, +0.000) and Holm-significant in 4. LightGBM from B to A rose to +0.035. Utility at the target-optimal threshold (S3; threshold searched on a grid of score quantiles) differed from utility at the transported threshold by ≤0.022 in absolute value for 15 of 16 transferred models (0.044 for PF logistic regression from A to B). Ranking rather than threshold transport therefore drove most of the utility loss.

### 3.6 Where the shift enters: additive decomposition
For the FULL fuzzy scorecard, the change in mean logit from source validation to target test was −0.25 to −0.33 (A→B) and +0.48 to +0.73 (B→A) across the five seeds. The additive decomposition reproduced these changes exactly. "Recently measured" and "frequently measured" terms accounted for 63–72% (A→B) and 68–75% (B→A) of the summed absolute contributions (Figure 3). The largest single contributors were the measurement recency of troponin I and FiO₂ (A→B) and of base excess and bicarbonate (B→A), mirroring the measurement-rate differences in §3.1. The models trained at A placed 12–14 measurement terms among their 20 most important terms, versus 4–7 for models trained at B. The top-20 sets overlapped moderately across seeds (mean Jaccard 0.69 for A, 0.67 for B).

### 3.7 Subgroups (descriptive, not pre-specified)
External AUROC of the FULL LightGBM was 0.765 in women and 0.794 in men (A→B), and 0.767 and 0.792 (B→A). Differences by age band (<65 vs ≥65 years) were smaller than 0.01 for LightGBM. No uncertainty intervals were computed for subgroups.

## 4. Discussion

**Principal findings.** Across four model classes and both transfer directions between two hospital systems, explicit observation-process features increased within-hospital discrimination but lost more of that discrimination at the other hospital than value-only inputs did. The effect was consistent in sign (7 of 8), clearly present for logistic regression and, from A to B, for the GRU, small for LightGBM, and absent or reversed in 2 of 8 combinations. In none of the four classes did process features buy better discrimination at the new hospital from A to B. Process features also accounted for the majority of the change in the model's average output between hospitals. When source-calibrated conformal sets were transported, they missed septic hours in one direction and flagged too many non-septic hours in the other.

**Relation to prior work.** Our findings extend, to hourly early-warning models, the association between measurement counts and domain shift reported for sepsis mortality prediction (Yamamoto et al., 2026). They are consistent with the observation that test-ordering processes carry prognostic information (Agniel et al., 2018). They offer one mechanism for the poor cross-hospital generalizability reported in the PhysioNet/CinC 2019 Challenge (Reyna et al., 2020): part of what these models learn is local measurement practice. Label-shift corrections such as BBSE and EM assume that the class-conditional input distribution is unchanged (Lipton et al., 2018; Saerens et al., 2002). Measurement-practice differences violate that assumption directly, which explains their failure here.

**Implications.** Developers who report internal gains from missingness indicators, time-since-measurement or measurement counts should not assume those gains carry to other sites. The comparison that matters for deployment is performance at the new site, where value-only models were as good or better in most of our comparisons. Distribution-free uncertainty sets calibrated at the development site offered no protection against this shift. Local recalibration with labeled data from the deployment site remains necessary.

**Strengths.**
- Verified official data that reproduce every published cohort count.
- Bidirectional transfer evaluated against locally trained models on identical patients.
- An equal, pre-specified tuning budget across four model classes.
- Patient-level resampling and patient-level conformal calibration that respect within-patient dependence.
- Multiplicity correction and a written analysis plan.

**Limitations.**
- Only two hospital systems were available, so each direction is a single transfer and the heterogeneity across directions cannot be explained.
- The analysis plan was written after an exploratory analysis of the same data. The primary estimand was not tested in that phase for logistic regression and the GRU, but the exploratory results may have influenced its choice.
- Process-free inputs still carry residual process information through last-observation-carried-forward values.
- Septic records end 8–10 hours after the label turns positive, a property of the Challenge data that shapes the relation between length of stay and outcome. Removing ICULOS did not change the conclusions.
- Logistic regression selected the boundary of its regularization grid, and the FULL fuzzy scorecard varied across seeds.
- Race and ethnicity are not available, so fairness could only be examined by sex and age.
- The label definition itself depends on clinical practice (antibiotic and culture orders), so hospitals may differ in outcome ascertainment as well as in inputs.
- Hourly performance metrics weight long stays more heavily than short ones.

**Conclusion.** Observation-process features are a double-edged input for sepsis early-warning models: they raise performance at the development site but that advantage does not survive transfer, they dominate the shift in model output between hospitals, and uncertainty sets calibrated at the development site do not correct for it. Evaluation at a second site, against a locally trained model on the same patients, should accompany any claim that process features improve such models.
